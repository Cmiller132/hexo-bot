Search & training — the same idea twice

One formula runs this whole system: take the network’s policy, re-weight it by what the critic knows, and you get a better policy. At play time the re-weighting is a short, sharp lookahead — Gumbel sequential halving over sixteen candidate moves. At training time it is the closed-form KLENT operator, and the network is pulled toward its own improvement, game after game. Both halves of this page run on real captured data from the served model.

01why search at all

On the featured position the raw policy’s favourite is (0, 1) at 27%. The search examined it, watched its line stall near 0, and cut it — while (−2, 6), the prior’s second choice at 7%, kept revealing value the deeper its line ran, finishing at +0.66 and taking the move. Four turns later the game ended with a six-in-a-row through that exact cell. The prior proposes; the search disposes.

02the mechanics

The search is Gumbel sequential halving over deterministic lines — not a branching tree. One move decision runs:

  1. Root read. One forward of the network gives the prior over all legal cells (fig 3 on the network page). The root evaluation is outside the simulation budget.
  2. Candidates. The top m = 16 cells by ln π + Gumbel noise become candidate lines. On this site the noise scale is zero — candidates are exactly the prior’s top 16 and every game against the same moves is reproducible.
  3. Deepening waves. Each surviving line extends one ply per wave: the network evaluates the line’s current position, and the line steps to that evaluation’s most probable reply — each line is a principal variation, played out move by most-expected move. The line’s value is the newest evaluation’s v̂, flipped into the root player’s frame. All of a wave’s positions are evaluated in one batched forward.
  4. Halving. After each round of waves, survivors are sorted by line value and the bottom half is cut. The budget is spent evenly across rounds, so later rounds probe half the lines twice as deep (fig 1).
  5. Decision. Among the final survivors, the move maximizing
    gumbel + ln π(a) + (50 + max_visits) · value(a)
    is played. The value weight grows with depth, so by the wire the searched values dominate the prior — a line that proved better wins even from a modest prior.

04training: KLENT self-play

The served checkpoint is iteration 402 of a KLENT self-play run. Each iteration plays 4,096 complete games against itself across 1,024 parallel environments, fits one epoch against that fresh buffer, then discards it — fully on-policy, no replay window. At every acting ply the improved policy is computed in closed form from the two heads:

π′(a)  ∝  exp[ (Q̃(a) + τ·ln π(a)) / (τ + λ) ]

τ keeps π′ anchored to the policy (entropy temper), λ is the KL leash that limits how far one improvement step may move, and Q̃ is the critic’s mass-normalized score from the heads section. The run trained with τ = 0.1, λ = 0.01 — and those exact values ride inside the checkpoint export, because π′ is meaningless under parameters the model did not train with. Play with the dials:

what the losses pull toward

Every acting ply writes one training row, and two losses fit it:

  • Policy cross-entropy against π′. The network’s next prior is pulled toward this ply’s improvement — the policy bootstraps on its own refinement.
  • Critic cross-entropy on the taken action. The target return G is a λ-return over v̂ (λret = 0.939, γ = 0.99), and the three-way target is (max(G,0), max(−G,0), 1−|G|) — a return of +0.6 teaches 60% win-mass, 40% nothing-mass. Because Hexo interleaves two placements per turn, consecutive steps flip perspective only at turn handovers: the return chain carries +1 within a turn, −1 across one (fig 4).

Nothing else is trained in self-play: the state-value head stays untouched (hence the v̂ note), there is no Dirichlet noise, no forced playouts, and no symmetry augmentation — the inputs already cannot tell symmetric positions apart. An evaluation harness plays the checkpoint against fixed opponents every 25 iterations, but it only observes; nothing gates or rolls back. Iteration 402 was picked for serving by pooled evaluation over the run’s late checkpoints.

05how this site serves it

The bot you play runs this exact stack on an Intel Arc A310 (4 GB), through PyTorch’s XPU backend in eager mode. Each search wave’s leaf positions go through one batched forward; a numeric parity self-check runs at every boot before the worker accepts games. The sims picker is the search budget of fig 1 — 16, 32, 64, or 128 simulations per stone. Opening noise is disabled site-wide, so the bot is deterministic: replay the same moves and you get the same game.

And the live viewer you can enable while playing is drawing the same frames as fig 2: the full-board fill is the policy prior, rings are the 16 candidates, each ring’s arc fills with its line’s share of the budget, the centre tick colours by the line’s current value, and a cell’s brightness tracks its live ranking. When a candidate dims and stays, you just watched a halving round cut it.
